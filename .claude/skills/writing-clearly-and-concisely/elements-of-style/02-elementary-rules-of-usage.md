# II. Elementary Rules of Usage

## 1. Form the possessive singular of nouns by adding 's

Follow this rule whatever the final consonant.

    Charles's friend
    Burns's poems
    the witch's malice

Exceptions: ancient proper names ending in *-es* and *-is*, and the possessives
*Jesus'*, *for conscience' sake*, *for righteousness' sake*.

The pronominal possessives *hers*, *its*, *theirs*, *yours*, *ours* have no
apostrophe. *It's* is a contraction of *it is*.

## 2. In a series of three or more terms with a single conjunction, use a comma after each term except the last

    red, white, and blue
    gold, silver, or copper
    He opened the letter, read it, and made a note of its contents.

In the abbreviation *etc.*, always place a comma before it.

## 3. Enclose parenthetic expressions between commas

    The best way to see a country, unless you are pressed for time, is to
    travel on foot.

This rule is difficult to apply because it is often hard to decide whether a
single word — such as *however* — or a brief phrase is or is not parenthetic. If
the interruption is slight, the commas may be omitted. But whether the
interruption is slight or considerable, never omit one comma and leave the other.

A non-restrictive relative clause is parenthetic and takes commas:

    In 1769, when Napoleon was born, Corsica had but recently been acquired
    by France.

Restrictive clauses take no commas:

    People who live in glass houses shouldn't throw stones.

Similar parenthetic constructions include the year in a date, an address, and
titles following a name:

    February to July, 1916
    Rural Free Delivery, No. 15, Franklin, Virginia
    James Wright, M.D.

Abbreviations such as *etc.* and *jr.* are always preceded by a comma and,
except at the end of a sentence, followed by one.

## 4. Place a comma before *and* or *but* introducing an independent clause

    The early records of the city have disappeared, and the story of its
    first years can no longer be reconstructed.

When the subject is the same for both clauses and is expressed only once, a
comma is used only if the connective is *but*. When the connective is *and*, the
comma should be omitted if the relation between the clauses is close.

    He has had several years' experience and is thoroughly competent.

## 5. Do not join independent clauses by a comma

If two or more grammatically complete clauses are not joined by a conjunction,
use a semicolon.

    Stevenson's romances are entertaining; they are full of exciting
    adventures.

You may also write them as separate sentences. If the second clause is preceded
by an adverb such as *accordingly*, *besides*, *then*, *therefore*, or *thus*,
and not by a conjunction, the semicolon is still required.

The exception: if the clauses are very short and alike in form, a comma is
usually permissible.

    Man proposes, God disposes.

## 6. Do not break sentences in two

Do not use periods for commas. Comma-spliced fragments — "He was an interesting
talker. A man who had traveled all over the world." — should be joined.

An occasional emphatic fragment is legitimate, but the writer should be certain
the emphasis is warranted.

## 7. A participial phrase at the beginning of a sentence must refer to the grammatical subject

    Walking slowly down the road, he saw a woman accompanied by two children.

Violations produce absurdity:

    Wrong:  On arriving in Chicago, his friends met him at the station.
    Right:  When he arrived in Chicago, his friends met him at the station.

This rule equally applies to gerund, infinitive, and elliptical phrases.

## 8. Divide words at line-ends in accordance with their formation and pronunciation

Divide between syllables: *know-ledge*, *Shake-speare*, *de-scribe*. Never
divide one-syllable words. Never leave a single letter on a line.
